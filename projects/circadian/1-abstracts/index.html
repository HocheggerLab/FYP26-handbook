    Part 1 · Abstract classification¶

    Goal¶

    Classify 300 abstracts against seven questions, by hand, to create an independent reference. The classifier (Jev) then answers the same questions for the same abstracts, and you measure how good it is compared with a human. Why this evaluation matters.

    Time¶

    About ten hours: two minutes an abstract once you are practised. Spread it over several sittings; label quality drops after an hour. Your supervisor sets the deadline.

    Before you start¶

    • Get CB1_items.json from your supervisor. It holds 300 shuffled abstracts: some about the circadian clock in breast cancer, some about other cancers, some about neither.
    • Keep AI out of this task, and do not open the full papers until your answers are locked.

    The seven questions¶

    The same questions, with the same wording, go to you and to the classifier.

    # Question Options
    1 Circadian: the abstract reports research on the circadian clock, circadian rhythms or circadian disruption (for example shift work or light at night) yes, no, unsure
    2 Cancer: the abstract reports research on cancer or tumours yes, no, unsure
    3 Breast cancer: the abstract reports research on breast cancer specifically yes, no, unsure
    4 Clock gene: the abstract reports results on at least one named core clock gene or protein: BMAL1 (ARNTL), CLOCK, PER1–3, CRY1–2, NR1D1–2 (REV-ERB) yes, no, unsure
    5 Evidence: the main kind of evidence reported patient_samples_or_cohort, cell_lines, animal_model, epidemiology, review, not_stated
    6 Expression data: the abstract reports gene or protein expression measured in human tumour or patient samples yes, no, unsure
    7 Full text: take the paper forward. The abstract reports original results that link at least one named core clock gene or protein to cancer: tumour growth or cancer cell behaviour, expression in tumours, patient outcome, or treatment response yes, no, unsure
    • Answer all seven for every abstract, including the ones that are not about the clock or cancer. A clear no is as useful to the evaluation as a yes.
    • unsure: the abstract does not settle it. Do not guess from what would make biological sense.
    • Full text: yes and unsure both queue the paper for full-text reading. Reviews and commentaries are no, and so is shift-work epidemiology that names no clock gene. Any cancer counts, not only breast.
    • epidemiology: population studies, such as shift work and cancer risk. If several kinds of evidence apply, choose the one the conclusions rest on.
    • The trap: "clock" in another sense. An epigenetic clock or mitotic clock is not the circadian clock. Melatonin counts as circadian only when the abstract frames it as a rhythm or clock signal.

    Steps¶

    1. Open the circadian annotation tool and load CB1_items.json.
    2. Read the title and abstract.
    3. Answer the seven questions.
    4. Export after every sitting. Keep the downloaded JSON: browser storage is not a backup.
    5. Check that the tool reports 300/300 complete, then send the final export to your supervisor.

    Resume from a saved export¶

    In the same browser, load CB1_items.json again to resume browser-saved work. On another browser, or after clearing storage:

    1. Load CB1_items.json.
    2. Click Restore saved annotations and choose your latest export.
    3. Check the record count, then confirm.

    Restore replaces current answers rather than merging them. Export current work first if you need to keep it.

    Done when¶

    • All 300 items have all seven answers.
    • The export reports 300/300 complete.
    • You have sent the final export and kept a local copy.
    • Your supervisor confirms it is locked before you see any classifier answers.

    What happens next¶

    Your supervisor runs the classifier on the same 300 abstracts. You then compare its answers with yours, question by question: where does it agree, where does it miss papers, where does it include papers it should not? Question 7 matters most: it decides which papers are read in full. You will also test whether asking it directly beats combining the simpler questions (cancer, clock gene, not a review). That comparison, and checking how many relevant papers the PubMed search itself misses, is Part 2.

    If you get stuck¶

    Use unsure when the abstract does not settle a question, and not_stated for evidence. Ask your supervisor about a convention if you need to; do not look up the paper or ask AI to resolve an answer.
