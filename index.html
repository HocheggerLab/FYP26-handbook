    FYP26 — AI agents in the biosciences¶

    Your task in this project is to design a degron tagging approach for a phosphatase gene, and to compare your own design with one produced by an AI agent. You will learn some basic biological concepts — gene targeting, CRISPR, induced degradation, phosphatases — and in parallel get exposed to agentic AI, its powers and its limitations.

    AI is sold as a research assistant. How good is it actually, on real scientific work?

    Nobody can answer that without a ground truth — a set of answers worked out carefully by a person, against which a model can be scored. Producing that ground truth is your project.

    You are not using AI. You are measuring it.

    The week 1 lecture, in full

    Download the introduction slides (PowerPoint, 39 slides) — the whole project in one place: the biology, the design decisions, how the agents are run and how any of it gets scored.

    Everything on this site is somewhere in that deck. Come back to it when a phase opens and you want to see where the piece you are working on fits.

    What you will do¶

    The project is structured in four phases to take you step by step through the different tasks. All five of you follow the same progression. The only thing that differs is which phosphatase you work on. You each get a different one, and everyone also does one shared protein. You find out which in week 4, when phase 1 rolls out on 27 October.

    At the end of phases 0, 1 and 2 you deliver an assignment and present it in a group meeting. Phase 3 ends with the thesis.

    Phase What Weeks Ends with
    0 · Background Degron tags and agentic AI — and the bridge between them 1–5 intro + talk, 3 Nov
    1 · Literature Your protein, and whether anyone has tagged it 4–7 annotations 10 Nov, protein intro 17 Nov
    2 · Cloning Design the guide and the knock-in donor from 24 Nov a guide and a donor
    3 · Analysis Score the models against your annotations; write up → the thesis

    Eleven weeks this term; the write-up is yours to pace over the vacation. The timeline has the detail.

    You answer first. Your answers are then locked, and only afterwards do the same questions go to the AI agents. Your work is the measuring stick, so it cannot be contaminated by seeing the model's answer.

    You get a Claude subscription¶

    Paid for by the department through Claude for Science, with a proper introduction to Claude Code. You will use it for reading, for code, for drafting, and for running your own agent over your gene.

    One exception, and it is the whole project: the 50 abstracts you annotate in phase 1 are due on 10 November, the day accounts are issued — so they are finished before you have an agent. Three hours of work, and it buys the only number here that nobody else could produce. Why.

    Why everyone does the shared protein too¶

    Five people answering identical questions on one protein shows how much two careful humans disagree. Without that number, "the agent scored 0.7" means nothing — there is no scale to read it against.

    It was picked as the one protein with a rich literature on both halves of the question: it has been degron-tagged and it is a well-known target for chemical degraders. A control with a thin answer on either half would waste the exercise.
