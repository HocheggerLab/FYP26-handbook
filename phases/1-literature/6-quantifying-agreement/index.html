    Part 6 · Quantifying the classification¶

    Goal¶

    Measure how well the classifier agreed with your own classifications, and produce your first result figure.

    This is the point of the whole exercise. Parts 2 and 5 produced two sets of answers about the same 50 abstracts; this part turns them into a number, a figure, and a judgement about whether the tool is good enough to use.

    Time¶

    About three hours, plus reading time for the disagreements.

    Before you start¶

    • Part 5 finished: you have results/jev_tagging_v3.jsonl.
    • Your own classifications submitted and locked.

    Steps¶

    1. Open the notebook¶

    In the fyp26-tools folder:

    uv run marimo edit notebooks/abstract_agreement.py

    That opens the notebook in a browser tab. The notebook is a stub: the scaffolding is written, the analysis is not. Cells marked TODO are yours to fill in.

    This is marimo, not Jupyter

    You may have used Jupyter before. marimo differs in three ways that matter today:

    • It is a .py file, not JSON. You can read it in any editor, and git can show you what changed. An .ipynb is a wall of JSON that no one can review.
    • It is reactive. Change a cell and everything that depends on it re-runs by itself. There is no "restart and run all", and no stale variable left over from a cell you deleted twenty minutes ago — which is the single commonest source of wrong results in a Jupyter notebook.
    • A variable may only be defined in one cell. Defining it twice is an error rather than a silent overwrite. If you want a second version of something, give it a new name.

    The first two are reproducibility properties. Your analysis has to survive someone else running it, including you in March.

    2. Look at the skill first¶

    Before you start filling cells in, open .claude/skills/fyp26-notebook/SKILL.md and read it. It is short.

    A skill is a folder of instructions the agent loads when it becomes relevant — here, when you start working on this notebook. It is how you give an agent procedural knowledge that is specific to your project: which commands to run, which conventions to follow, and what it must not do. CLAUDE.md (Part 5) is always in context; a skill is loaded only when needed, so you can afford to make it detailed.

    Two things to notice as you read it:

    • It contains a "Do not" section. Telling an agent what not to do is at least as important as telling it what to do — and in this notebook one of those lines is "do not fill in all the TODOs at once and hand back a finished notebook", which is a constraint written against your own short-term convenience.
    • The frontmatter description is what the agent matches on. If it were vague, the skill would never load at the right moment.

    Ask your agent: "what does the fyp26-notebook skill tell you to do, and how does that differ from how you would approach this notebook without it?"

    3. Work through it with the agent¶

    Use the agent the way you would use a demonstrator: ask it to explain an approach, to suggest how to structure a comparison, to help when something errors.

    You must be able to explain every step

    You will describe this analysis in your report, and in the viva. If a cell runs and you cannot say what it does and why, you have not finished that cell. A good test: close the laptop and explain the step out loud. If you cannot, ask the agent to explain it differently rather than moving on.

    Useful prompts: "explain what this cell does, line by line", "why is this the right way to join these two tables?", "what would go wrong if I did it the other way?"

    4. The three things that need a decision¶

    The notebook will not make these for you, and each belongs in your methods.

    Joining the two tables. Your annotations use item_id (s1_003); the model output uses pmid. The corpus file carries both. Check the row count after joining: if it is not 50, something was silently dropped, and that is the commonest way an analysis like this goes wrong.

    The skip rule. When you answered no to is_tagging_paper you left the other three fields blank. The classifier answers all four questions every time, independently, so it produced a tag_type even for papers it called no. Compare those and you mark the model wrong on fields you left blank. Decide how to handle it and say so.

    What counts as agreement for a field with more than two options. Is unclear versus other as wrong as AID versus dTag?

    5. Do not stop at an accuracy figure¶

    Your 50 abstracts were drawn deliberately: roughly 8 tagging papers, 2 ambiguous, 40 negatives. A classifier that answered no to everything would score about 80% — and be useless.

    So report the confusion matrix, not just the headline number, and separate the two kinds of mistake: tagging papers it missed, and false alarms it raised. For a tool that decides what you read in full, those costs are not equal. Say which matters more here, and why.

    6. Read the disagreements¶

    For every abstract where you and the classifier differed, open it and decide: were you right, was it right, or is the abstract genuinely ambiguous?

    This is the most valuable half-hour in the project. It is also the part that cannot be delegated — you are the reference, and the point is to find out where the reference itself is soft. A disagreement where you turn out to be wrong is not a failure of the experiment. It is the experiment working.

    7. Make the figure¶

    One figure answering: how well did the classifier reproduce my classifications? It must stand alone — labelled axes, the n stated, and a caption saying what was compared and on how many abstracts. Save PNG and PDF into figures/.

    8. Log it¶

    An ai-use-log.md entry: what you asked, what came back, what you had to fix or reject. Code the agent wrote needs the declaration comment from the ground rules, and a note of how you checked it.

    Done when¶

    • Every cell in the notebook runs clean (marimo re-runs dependents for you, so a green notebook means the whole graph is consistent).
    • You can state the agreement on is_tagging_paper, with the confusion matrix behind it.
    • You have read every disagreement and classified it as your error, the model's error, or a genuinely ambiguous abstract.
    • One figure saved in figures/.
    • The five questions at the end of the notebook are answered in prose.
